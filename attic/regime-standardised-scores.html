Standardising forecast scores by epidemic regime

Exploratory sketch. Not part of the main manuscript.

The problem

Forecast evaluations report a mean score per target type, and compare those means. Cases and deaths are scored separately, and deaths usually score better.

That mean is taken over whatever mix of epidemic conditions the evaluation period happened to contain. If score depends on epidemic state, and the state distribution differs between the things being compared, the comparison is confounded by the mix rather than by anything about forecasting.

This is the structure of a crude rate comparison, and epidemiology already has the tool for it.

The analogy carries the diagnostic as well as the method. When stratum-specific rates are proportional across groups, a single standardised summary is informative. When they cross or diverge, the summary hides the thing worth reporting, and the strata should be shown instead.

Data and stratification

Weekly COVID-19 case and death forecasts submitted to the European Forecast Hub, scored with the weighted interval score on the log scale (LWIS), horizon one only. The Hub ensemble is excluded.

Epidemic regime is the weekly log change in observed incidence, cut at ±0.05 and ±0.2, computed within country and outcome. Growth is dimensionless, so its strata mean the same thing for cases and deaths. Incidence level is not comparable across outcomes and is not used for stratification here; the modelled extension below handles it.

Reading the result

Compare Table 2 with Table 4 to see how much of the crude gap is regime mix. Then read the ratio column of Table 3 to see whether a single standardised number is defensible at all.

A roughly constant ratio across regimes means the outcomes differ by a common factor, and either the crude or the standardised comparison summarises it. A ratio that changes across regimes means the difference between outcomes depends on epidemic state, and no single number represents it. In that case the standardised value is arithmetically correct and substantively misleading, in the same way an age-standardised rate ratio is when the age-specific ratios cross.

Model-based standardisation

Direct standardisation on a single stratifying variable stops where the strata get thin, and cannot hold incidence level, country, and variant constant at the same time. The model-based equivalent is g-computation: fit score as a function of the target-generating variables, predict every target under a common regime, and average.

This holds incidence level, country, and variant at their observed values while moving only growth, so it separates epidemic phase from the level and place it usually travels with.

Limitations

Exploratory. No pre-specified hypothesis, and the regime cutpoints were chosen for interpretability rather than derived.

LWIS mixes calibration and sharpness, so a higher score means a forecast was scored worse, not that the target was harder in any independent sense.

Growth is measured on observed data as reported, so revisions and reporting artefacts enter the stratification. Deaths lag cases, so a death week classified as stable may follow a volatile case week; the regimes are contemporaneous with the target, not with the epidemic.

Model composition is not adjusted for. If the set of models forecasting in one regime differs from another, regime-specific means absorb that difference. Adding s(Model, bs = "re") to m_target tests it, at the cost of no longer being a purely target-side description.

Horizon one only. If regime effects work through turning points, they should grow with horizon, which is a check rather than an assumption.
